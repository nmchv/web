I study geography, economics, and other areas of knowledge, teach, experiment with technology, and create my own digital tools. It is important for me not only to know how something works, but also why it works that way. And if existing solutions do not meet my needs, I prefer to try to create my own.

## Knowing the world as a way of interacting with it

I am convinced that the world around us cannot be fully understood by viewing it through the lens of only one discipline. Many processes lie at the intersection of different fields of knowledge, and it is often there that the most interesting patterns are discovered.

Geography helps us explore the spatial organization of the world and the interaction of natural and social processes. Economics helps us understand the patterns of economic activity and the distribution of resources. Psychology opens the door to studying human thought and behavior. Information technology gives us tools for data analysis, process automation, and new ways of working with information. I am interested not only in going deeper into individual topics, but also in finding connections among them. How do natural conditions shape the economy? How do aspects of human thinking shape the learning process? How can technology change the ways we gain knowledge? Why do some systems prove effective while others fail to meet their goals? Questions like these require a comprehensive approach. Answers are rarely found within a single discipline, so it is important for me to continually broaden my horizons and learn new research tools.

I strive for broad knowledge not for the sake of accumulating facts. Much more important is to see the whole picture, understand the relationships, and be able to use what I have learned to solve new problems. For me, learning does not end with mastering a certain subject or earning a degree. It is an ongoing process in which each new field of knowledge can change the way I understand something that once seemed clear.

## Information, knowledge, and understanding

I pay a great deal of attention to how information works. In the modern world, access to information on almost any topic has become easier. But the availability of information does not automatically mean that a person understands the subject. One can memorize many facts, know definitions, and reproduce prepared explanations while still being unable to work through a new situation independently. For me, information, knowledge, and understanding are different levels of working with content. Information provides the raw material. Knowledge allows us to systematize it, describe phenomena, and use accumulated experience. Understanding emerges when, beyond isolated facts, a system becomes visible: relationships between elements, causal links, fundamental principles, and patterns of what is happening. That is why I am interested not only in collecting information, but also in identifying what matters most, building structure, and finding explanations that enable further progress. I try to understand a topic well enough to separate fundamental ideas from secondary details, see the logic of the process being studied, and explain complex things in clear language. This approach is useful in teaching, research, and digital product development. In each case, I have to solve the same problem: make sense of a large amount of information, decide what really matters, and turn it into a clear and workable system. At the same time, I do not believe that understanding necessarily means having a final answer to every question. If a solution has not yet been found, that is a reason to continue the investigation, test assumptions, and look for a different approach.

## Geography as the foundation of my professional path

Geography has become my main professional field. I am drawn to its applied nature: it studies the world around us and allows us to consider real processes rather than abstract models alone. Nature, population, economy, territories, resources, and the interaction between people and the environment are all parts of a single system that can be studied, analyzed, and explained. Geography also interests me because it lies at the intersection of the natural and social sciences. It requires understanding natural patterns, taking human activity into account, working with spatial data, and establishing links between phenomena. This approach matches my view of the world: individual facts gain real value when they become part of a larger picture. From 2022 to 2027, I am studying at the Southern Federal University in the teacher education program with two training profiles: “Geography” and “Economics.” This combination allows me to look at the world from different angles. Geographic training gives me tools for researching territories and spatial processes, while economics helps me examine economic activity, resources, and the patterns of social development. I do not consider these two fields as isolated. On the contrary, I believe it is important to use knowledge from both disciplines to gain a fuller understanding of real-world problems. In the future, I plan to continue teaching and research. One of my long-term goals is to apply to a PhD program at a university in the United States and continue developing in academia. For me, this is a way to deepen my knowledge, engage in research, and continue working on questions that cannot be answered by simply reproducing what is already known.

## Education as the development of thinking

At the moment, I work as a geography teacher at MAOU “School No. 116” in Rostov-on-Don. In addition to geography, I also teach robotics and 3D modeling. Working in school allows me to face questions that go beyond a single subject every day. How do you explain a difficult topic? How do you help a student work through a problem independently? How do you organize material so it does not turn into a set of disconnected facts? How do you teach a person to apply knowledge in situations they have not encountered before? These questions shape my approach to education.

I believe the purpose of school is not only to pass on information and prepare students for tests. It is equally important to develop the ability to think for oneself, analyze, connect ideas, evaluate information, and look for solutions. I am close to an approach that assumes a move from simple memorization to more complex intellectual actions. In pedagogy, this idea is well captured by Bloom's taxonomy, which describes different levels of cognitive activity. Memorization and reproduction are necessary: without basic knowledge, it is impossible to move forward. But they should not become the ultimate goal of learning. It is important that a student be able to apply what they have learned, analyze information, evaluate solutions, and create their own ideas based on acquired knowledge. This is exactly the outcome I strive for in my work. I want students, after studying a topic, not only to repeat the teacher's explanation, but also to understand how to use what they have learned, where to look for additional information, and how to act when there is no ready-made answer. It is important to me to help students master complex topics, build a system of knowledge, and develop independence in learning. For me, a good educational result is not only a correctly completed task, but also the emergence in a person of the ability to solve new problems without constant reliance on ready-made instructions. I also see technology as a tool for the development of education. Digital services, interactive tasks, models, and feedback systems can make learning more visual and convenient. However, technology alone does not replace thinking. Its value is determined by how well it helps achieve educational goals.

## Beyond a single discipline

Although geography occupies a central place in my professional training, my interests are not limited to teaching a single subject. I am drawn to economics, psychology, information technology, and other fields that allow me to explore the world in different ways and solve practical problems. I consider interdisciplinary thinking to be one of the most important parts of my professional life. The ability to look at a problem from different angles helps reveal what is easy to miss in a narrow specialization. For example, an educational task can be examined from pedagogical, psychological, and technological perspectives at the same time. To create an effective learning tool, it is not enough to simply move material into a digital format. One must understand what it should teach, what actions the user performs, where difficulties may arise, and how to make interaction with the tool more convenient. Such tasks require a combination of knowledge and skills from different fields. I enjoy learning new directions, understanding unfamiliar subjects, and applying what I have learned where it may be useful. I do not try to artificially limit my interests to a single profession. At the same time, breadth of interest does not mean abandoning depth. On the contrary, I consider it important to combine broad knowledge with the ability to investigate specific questions in detail. The ability to see the whole picture and, when necessary, dive into the details is one of the principles that guides my work.

## Creating my own tools

In addition to teaching and studying different areas of knowledge, I create my own digital products. My motivation is quite practical: I need tools that match my tasks, work habits, and idea of convenience. Existing solutions can be useful, but they do not always allow me to organize work exactly as I need. In such situations, I prefer not to limit myself to finding a suitable service, but to try to create my own. This approach gradually led me to develop educational tools and to form the idea of a personal digital ecosystem. For me, creating software is an opportunity to organize a process on my own, think through the structure, define the necessary functions, and get a tool that actually solves a specific problem. It is important for me to understand how the system works, why it is designed the way it is, and how it can be improved. I prefer to control the entire project myself and understand its inner workings, even if that requires extra effort. In this process, I am equally attracted by practical problem-solving, the intellectual challenge, and the chance to bring my own ideas to life. I actively use modern AI tools in my work on digital projects. For me, AI is a universal assistant that can take part in research, analysis, design, and development. I am especially interested in the possibility of creating working products while gradually mastering the necessary technologies and testing ideas in practice. What interests me is not only the result of the development. The process itself is also important: understand the task, choose an approach, assemble a system from individual components, test how it works, and bring it to a state in which it is truly useful. I see programming and AI primarily as tools for expanding my own capabilities. They allow me to move from an idea to a working solution and experiment with tasks that would previously have required other resources.

## Teacher's diary

One of my projects is a digital teacher’s diary that I am developing based on my own professional needs. A teacher’s work consists not only of giving lessons. It also includes planning, keeping records, documenting learning outcomes, working with grades and attendance, organizing homework, and many other tasks. When the tools for this work do not match real needs, one has to adapt to their limitations or look for additional solutions. I chose a different path and decided to create my own tool. My goal is to bring the necessary work functions into a single system so that daily activity becomes more organized and unnecessary actions are reduced. For me, this project is an example of a practical approach to technology: the starting point is a real problem, and the result is a tool created to solve it. In the future, such developments may also be useful to other teachers who need flexible tools for organizing the learning process.

## Geography trainer

Another project I am working on is a geography trainer for studying and testing geographical knowledge. Geography requires not only understanding processes, but also mastery of a great deal of specific information: geographical objects, their locations, the features of territories, and the relative positions of different elements of the earth’s surface. Practice is necessary for mastering this material. At the same time, checking knowledge does not necessarily have to be reduced to repetitive tasks with an unchanged set of questions. I am developing the geography trainer as a tool that allows training geographical knowledge and adapting tasks to specific learning goals. I am interested in creating systems in which subject matter is combined with the capabilities of digital technologies. This approach makes working with educational material more flexible and gives students an additional opportunity for independent preparation. In the future, individual educational tools may become part of a larger system that combines different learning formats and practices. For me, the geography trainer is not just a technical project. It is an example of how a concrete educational task can be addressed by means of a self-developed tool.

## Personal digital ecosystem

The teacher’s diary and the geography trainer are separate projects, but behind them lies a broader idea. I want to gradually form my own digital environment in which I can organize my work, store and structure information, investigate the questions that interest me, and create new tools. It is important to me that the different elements of this system help solve real tasks rather than exist solely for the sake of development itself. In the first place, the ecosystem is created for me. It should match my working style, support my interests, and allow me to quickly move from one task to another. At the same time, I do not exclude the possibility that some solutions may also be useful to other people. If a tool can effectively solve not only my task but also similar problems for other users, it can be developed further — including as a standalone digital product. That is why I see my own developments both as a personal working environment and as a potential foundation for future projects. I like the idea of building the tools I use every day myself, understanding how they work, and gradually expanding their capabilities. In the end, what matters to me is not the scale of the system in itself, but how well it helps me perform tasks and opens new possibilities for work.

## Freedom and independence

Freedom occupies a central place in my understanding of life. For me, it is connected with the ability to choose directions for development for myself, manage my time, set priorities, and organize my activities according to my own interests and goals. I do not want to limit myself to a single field of knowledge, a single professional path, or a pre-defined idea of what a person with a particular education should do. It is important for me to keep the ability to learn new things, change direction, try different approaches, and create my own solutions. In this sense, independence is not only a principle for me, but also a practical approach. If I need a specific tool, I can try to develop it. If a task requires knowledge I do not yet have, I can begin studying the relevant area. If an existing system does not meet my requirements, I can explore alternatives. Such an approach requires time and effort, but it allows me not to depend exclusively on existing solutions. At the same time, independence does not mean that I consider all my own solutions to be ideal. I understand that any system can be improved, and that initial ideas about a task may be incomplete. That is why analysis, checking results, and being ready to seek a different approach when the previous one does not work are so important to me. I try to organize my life so that I have the opportunity to do what I consider interesting and meaningful and to decide for myself where to direct my efforts.

In 2026, I took part in the academic exchange program at Suwon University in South Korea. This experience allowed me to spend some time in a different educational and cultural environment, live in unfamiliar surroundings, and interact with people in another language. For me, it was not only a continuation of my studies, but also an opportunity to gain new experience, broaden my understanding of the world, and observe how educational processes are organized in another country. What proved especially valuable was the combination of personal experience and professional observation. Being in a different environment makes it possible to look at familiar things in a new way, notice features that had previously gone unnoticed, and compare different approaches to organizing daily life and learning. In the same year, I participated in the international forum **2026 YSI-KOICA SDGs and Sustainable Futures Forum**, devoted to sustainable development and the future. Participation in such international educational initiatives broadens the range of questions under discussion and allows me to learn about topics at the intersection of science, education, and social development. I see this kind of experience as part of continuous learning — an opportunity to step outside the familiar environment and gain additional perspectives on the processes that interest me.

## Continuing the path

At the moment I combine university study, teaching, and the development of my own digital projects. These areas require different kinds of knowledge and skills, but together they allow me to grow in several fields that interest me at once. In the coming years, I plan to continue my teaching and research work, develop my projects, and expand my professional competencies. In a longer-term perspective, I am considering an academic career and studying for a PhD in the United States. At the same time, I do not try to determine every stage of the future in advance. What matters more to me is preserving the ability to choose new directions based on accumulated experience, emerging interests, and opportunities that arise. I continue to study the world, learn new tools, and look for solutions to tasks I consider interesting. Perhaps this is what best unites everything I do: the desire to understand how things work, see the links between individual elements, and create something that allows this understanding to be put into practice. For me, it is important not only to observe how the world works, but also to find ways to interact with it meaningfully — through knowledge, research, teaching, and creating my own tools. I do not think this process can ever be fully completed. New questions give rise to new studies, new tasks require new solutions, and the fields of knowledge I have already learned open up opportunities to explore the next ones. That is why I continue to learn, research, and create. It is from these processes that my professional path is formed.
